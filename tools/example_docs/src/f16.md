# F-16 open loop

The nonlinear F-16 of Stevens & Lewis (*Aircraft Control and Simulation*, Section 3.6), on the low-speed wind-tunnel data of NASA TP-1538. Three aircraft are trimmed at the book's point, 502 ft/s (153 m/s) at sea level, with the centre of gravity at 0.30, 0.35 and 0.38 of the mean chord, and each is released on its own mode. The further aft the CG, the less the airframe weathercocks into the wind: at 0.38 it is statically unstable.

# The airframe

## Rigid body

The book's mass is 636.94 slug (9295.4 kg, weight 20 500 lb) with $J_{xx} = 9496$, $J_{yy} = 55\,814$, $J_{zz} = 63\,100$ and $J_{xz} = 982$ slug ft². The product of inertia tilts the principal axes by

$$\varepsilon = \tfrac12 \arctan\frac{2 J_{xz}}{J_{zz} - J_{xx}} = 1.05^\circ$$

about the body y axis. The stdlib `RigidBody3D` takes a diagonal inertia, so it sits on the principal axes; a `FixedTransform3DNeutral` (the Mount) carries the wrench from the geometric body frame (x forward, y left, z up) to them. `Free3D` gives the six degrees of freedom, gravity is part of `RigidBody3D`.

## Air data and coefficients

`F16AirData` turns the principal-axis pose and twist into the book's variables: $V_t$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, the body rates $p, q, r$ (x forward, y right, z down), the Euler angles, the altitude and the book's atmosphere (Mach and $\bar q$). The NASA tables are stdlib `Lookup2D` blocks over ($\alpha$, $\delta_e$) for $C_X$ and $C_m$, over ($\alpha$, $\beta$) for $C_l$, $C_n$ and the aileron and rudder derivatives, and `Lookup1D` over $\alpha$ for $C_Z$ and the nine damping derivatives. Breakpoints were added where the book extrapolates (elevator ±25 deg, a signed $\beta$ axis) so that bilinear interpolation reproduces the book's function exactly.

`F16Forces` builds the coefficients up as the book does,

$$C_{Z,T} = C_Z(\alpha)\Big(1 - (\beta/57.3)^2\Big) - 0.19\,\frac{\delta_e}{25} + \frac{\bar c\, q}{2 V_t} C_{Z_q}(\alpha), \qquad C_{m,T} = C_m + C_{Z,T}(x_{cg,r} - x_{cg}) + \dots$$

and the engine (`F16Engine`) is the F100 model with the throttle gearing, the power-level lag and the thrust tables over altitude and Mach. The engine's angular momentum (160 slug ft²/s) adds a gyroscopic moment.

## Checks against the book

The reference implementation `design/f16_ref.py` reproduces the book's check point (Table 3.5-2) to 3.9e-7 relative in all 13 state derivatives and the trims of Table 3.6-3 to every published digit (the [overview](page:overview) lists them). The bg-sim model matches it in turn (next page).

# Design

## Law

With $\alpha$ in deg, $q$ and $q_{cmd}$ in deg/s and the elevator $u$ in deg:

$$u = K_\alpha \alpha + K_q (q - k_{ff} q_{cmd}) + \xi, \qquad \dot\xi = K_i (q - q_{cmd}) + \frac{\mathrm{sat}(u) - u}{T_{aw}}$$

with $k_{ff} = 0.5$ and $T_{aw} = 0.1$ s. The integral accumulates $K_i$ times the error, so gains that change with the flight condition do not make the output jump.

## Gains

At every point of a 5 × 5 grid (qbar 80, 200, 400, 800, 1400 psf; Mach 0.2 to 1.0) `design/fbw.py` trims the aircraft at the altitude where that Mach gives that dynamic pressure, linearises it, and solves an LQR problem on the short-period states plus the integral of the pitch-rate error, with weights $Q = \mathrm{diag}(0.1, 0.5, 30)$ and $R = (M_\delta/6)^2$. Normalising $R$ by the elevator's pitch effectiveness $M_\delta$ makes the weights mean the same everywhere, and the gains fall roughly as $1/\bar q$: from $K_q = 3.2$ at 80 psf to 0.08 at 1400 psf.

Checked on the full linear model with the servo lag at every grid point: all closed-loop poles left of $-0.068$, pitch-rate step rise 0.21 to 0.60 s, overshoot at most 5.2 %. The book's data have no Mach effects, yet the Mach axis matters: at the same $\bar q$, a higher Mach means a higher true airspeed, and the lift and damping terms scale with $\bar q / V_t$.

At 900 ft/s (Mach 0.82) and 5000 ft the pilot pulls the stick fully aft from 1 s and lights the afterburner. The stick gearing asks for 11 g. **Protected** has the alpha/g limiter on; **Unprotected** has it off.

$$q_{stick} = \frac{g_0}{V_t}\big(1 + 10\,\delta_{stick} - \cos\gamma\cos\phi\big), \quad q_{cmd} = \min\Big(q_{stick},\; 3(25 - \alpha),\; \frac{g_0}{V_t}(9 - \cos\gamma\cos\phi) + 4 (9 - n_z)\Big)$$

so the commanded pitch rate fades out as alpha approaches 25 deg or the load factor 9 g.

# Pilot-induced oscillation

A category II PIO: an aggressive pilot captures a 7 deg pitch-attitude change at 500 ft/s, 15 000 ft. The pilot is a gain of 3 (deg/s of pitch-rate command per deg of attitude error) acting on what he saw 0.25 s ago. Small corrections stay linear and well damped. A large one drives the stabilator into its 60 deg/s rate limit, and the rate limit adds phase lag that the pilot loop cannot afford.

# Rate-limit compensation

A plain rate limiter follows its command at the maximum rate until it catches up. When the command reverses, the output keeps going until the command crosses it: that delay is the phase lag of the limiter, and it grows with the amplitude. The compensated limiter

$$\dot y = \mathrm{clamp}\Big(\frac{du}{dt} + k (u - y),\; \pm 60\ \mathrm{deg/s}\Big)$$

reverses as soon as the command does (the derivative term, through a `FilteredDerivative` with $T_f$ = 0.01 s); the slow pull-back $k = 0.5$ 1/s brings the output back onto the command afterwards. It gives up some amplitude for much less phase lag, the idea behind the phase-compensated rate limiter Saab introduced in the Gripen's flight control after its PIO accidents.
