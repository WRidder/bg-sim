The nonlinear F-16 of Stevens & Lewis (*Aircraft Control and Simulation*, Section 3.6), on the low-speed wind-tunnel data of NASA TP-1538. # The airframe

## Rigid body

The book's mass is 636.94 slug (9295.4 kg, weight 20 500 lb) with $J_{xx} = 9496$, $J_{yy} = 55\,814$, $J_{zz} = 63\,100$ and $J_{xz} = 982$ slug ft². The product of inertia tilts the principal axes by

$$\varepsilon = \tfrac12 \arctan\frac{2 J_{xz}}{J_{zz} - J_{xx}} = 1.05^\circ$$

about the body y axis. The stdlib `RigidBody3D` takes a diagonal inertia, so it sits on the principal axes; a `FixedTransform3DNeutral` (the Mount) carries the wrench from the geometric body frame (x forward, y left, z up) to them. `Free3D` gives the six degrees of freedom, gravity is part of `RigidBody3D`.

## Air data and coefficients

`F16AirData` turns the principal-axis pose and twist into the book's variables: $V_t$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, the body rates $p, q, r$ (x forward, y right, z down), the Euler angles, the altitude and the book's atmosphere (Mach and $\bar q$). The NASA tables are stdlib `Lookup2D` blocks over ($\alpha$, $\delta_e$) for $C_X$ and $C_m$, over ($\alpha$, $\beta$) for $C_l$, $C_n$ and the aileron and rudder derivatives, and `Lookup1D` over $\alpha$ for $C_Z$ and the nine damping derivatives. Breakpoints were added where the book extrapolates (elevator ±25 deg, a signed $\beta$ axis) so that bilinear interpolation reproduces the book's function exactly.

`F16Forces` builds the coefficients up as the book does,

$$C_{Z,T} = C_Z(\alpha)\Big(1 - (\beta/57.3)^2\Big) - 0.19\,\frac{\delta_e}{25} + \frac{\bar c\, q}{2 V_t} C_{Z_q}(\alpha), \qquad C_{m,T} = C_m + C_{Z,T}(x_{cg,r} - x_{cg}) + \dots$$

and the engine (`F16Engine`) is the F100 model with the throttle gearing, the power-level lag and the thrust tables over altitude and Mach. The engine's angular momentum (160 slug ft²/s) adds a gyroscopic moment.

## Checks against the book

The reference implementation `design/f16_ref.py` reproduces the book's check point (Table 3.5-2) to 3.9e-7 relative in all 13 state derivatives and the trims of Table 3.6-3 to every published digit (the [overview](page:overview) lists them). The bg-sim model matches it in turn (next page).

# Design

## Law

With $\alpha$ in deg, $q$ and $q_{cmd}$ in deg/s and the elevator $u$ in deg:

$$u = K_\alpha \alpha + K_q (q - k_{ff} q_{cmd}) + \xi, \qquad \dot\xi = K_i (q - q_{cmd}) + \frac{\mathrm{sat}(u) - u}{T_{aw}}$$

with $k_{ff} = 0.5$ and $T_{aw} = 0.1$ s. The integral accumulates $K_i$ times the error, so gains that change with the flight condition do not make the output jump.

## Gains

At every point of a 5 × 5 grid (qbar 80, 200, 400, 800, 1400 psf; Mach 0.2 to 1.0) `design/fbw.py` trims the aircraft at the altitude where that Mach gives that dynamic pressure, linearises it, and solves an LQR problem on the short-period states plus the integral of the pitch-rate error, with weights $Q = \mathrm{diag}(0.1, 0.5, 30)$ and $R = (M_\delta/6)^2$. Normalising $R$ by the elevator's pitch effectiveness $M_\delta$ makes the weights mean the same everywhere, and the gains fall roughly as $1/\bar q$: from $K_q = 3.2$ at 80 psf to 0.08 at 1400 psf.

Checked on the full linear model with the servo lag at every grid point: all closed-loop poles left of $-0.068$, pitch-rate step rise 0.21 to 0.60 s, overshoot at most 5.2 %. The book's data have no Mach effects, yet the Mach axis matters: at the same $\bar q$, a higher Mach means a higher true airspeed, and the lift and damping terms scale with $\bar q / V_t$.

# Rate-limit compensation

A plain rate limiter follows its command at the maximum rate until it catches up. When the command reverses, the output keeps going until the command crosses it: that delay is the phase lag of the limiter, and it grows with the amplitude. The compensated limiter

$$\dot y = \mathrm{clamp}\Big(\frac{du}{dt} + k (u - y),\; \pm 60\ \mathrm{deg/s}\Big)$$

reverses as soon as the command does (the derivative term, through a `FilteredDerivative` with $T_f$ = 0.01 s); the slow pull-back $k = 0.5$ 1/s brings the output back onto the command afterwards. It gives up some amplitude for much less phase lag, the idea behind the phase-compensated rate limiter Saab introduced in the Gripen's flight control after its PIO accidents.

# Control laws

Angles are in deg, rates in deg/s, surfaces in deg. The book's signs apply: a positive aileron rolls left and a positive rudder yaws left.

## Pitch

The stick commands a load factor: 10 g per unit aft and 4 g per unit forward. A turn compensation $tc = 1/\cos\phi - 1$ (faded out beyond 60 deg of bank) adds the load factor of a level turn, so a centred stick holds the altitude in a bank:

$$n_{cmd} = 1 + 10\,\delta_{stick} + tc, \qquad q_{stick} = \frac{g_0}{V_t}\Big(\Delta n_{cmd} + 2\,\mathrm{clamp}\big(\Delta n_{cmd} - \Delta n, \pm 0.5\big)\Big)$$

where $\Delta n_{cmd} = n_{cmd} - \cos\gamma\cos\phi$ and $\Delta n = n_z - \cos\theta\cos\phi$ (zero in steady straight flight and in a steady level turn). The first term is the pitch rate of the steady manoeuvre. The g-error term builds the load factor in about half a second instead of the airframe's path time constant. In the 60 deg turn below it cuts the altitude loss over 30 s from about 160 m to 45 m. The clamp keeps a full-stick pull from overdriving the alpha limiter. The limiter of `f16-envelope` follows, with a −3 g floor: $q_{cmd} = \max(\min(q_{stick}, q_\alpha, q_g), q_{neg})$. The law is the scheduled `PitchFBW` of `f16-fbw`.

## Roll

The stick commands the stability-axis roll rate $p_s = p\cos\alpha + r\sin\alpha$, a roll about the velocity vector:

$$p_{cmd} = p_{lim}\,\delta_{roll}, \qquad p_{lim} = 240\ \mathrm{clamp}\Big(\frac{\bar q}{12\ \mathrm{kPa}}, 0.3, 1\Big)\ \mathrm{clamp}\big(1 - 0.075 (n_z - 1), 0.4, 1\big)$$

$$\delta_a = K_p (p_s - 0.5\,p_{cmd}) + \xi_p, \qquad \dot\xi_p = K_{ip} (p_s - p_{cmd}) + \frac{\mathrm{sat}(\delta_a) - \delta_a}{0.1}$$

so the limit is 210 deg/s at 1 g and 500 ft/s and falls with the load factor and at low dynamic pressure. $K_p$ and $K_{ip}$ place the roll loop at 4 rad/s with damping 0.8 on the roll-subsidence model $\dot p = L_p p + L_{\delta a}\delta_a$ of each grid point (`design/lateral.py`).

## Yaw

$$\delta_r = K_{ari}\,\delta_a + K_r\, r_{wo} + K_\beta (\beta - \beta_{cmd}) + \xi_\beta, \qquad \dot\xi_\beta = K_{i\beta} (\beta - \beta_{cmd}) + \frac{\mathrm{sat}(\delta_r) - \delta_r}{0.1}$$

- $r_{wo}$ is the stability-axis yaw rate through a 1 s washout, so the yaw damper does not fight a steady turn.
- The sideslip integral holds $\beta = \beta_{cmd} = -6\,\delta_{pedal}$ deg, so a turn with the pedals centred is coordinated.
- The aileron-rudder interconnect $K_{ari}(\alpha) = (\tan\alpha\, L'_{\delta a} - N'_{\delta a}) / (N'_{\delta r} - \tan\alpha\, L'_{\delta r})$ cancels the yaw that the ailerons cause about the velocity vector. It runs from −0.53 at 0 deg alpha to 1.9 at 20 deg, clamped to [−1, 2].

$K_r$, $K_\beta$ and $K_{i\beta}$ come from a joint optimisation at each grid point: the integrated error of a sideslip step, plus the sideslip of a roll, with a penalty when the dutch-roll damping falls below 0.6. Over the 5 × 5 grid (qbar 80..1400 psf, Mach 0.2..1.0) the full law gives a dutch-roll damping of at least 0.6, and a roll-rate step rises in 0.35..0.55 s.

# Results

All numbers come from `design/simscen.py`, a scipy simulation of the same airframe and laws. bg-sim checks against it in `expected.json`.

## Coordinated 60 deg turn (the default run)

![bank, sideslip, load factor and altitude|900](@fig:turn)

A 0.284 roll stick for 1 s banks the aircraft to 60 deg, and the throttle goes to 0.33 to hold the speed at 2 g. The sideslip stays below 0.6 deg and the speed within 1 m/s. A centred stick commands the load factor of a level turn, not an altitude, so the path sags slowly: 19 m after 10 s, 45 m after 30 s.

## Roll-rate step

![roll rate, surfaces and sideslip|900](@fig:roll)

A quarter stick commands 52.6 deg/s. The stability-axis roll rate reaches 90 % of it 0.57 s after the stick moves and overshoots by 2 %. A full-stick roll at the same point holds 217 deg/s against a limit of 219 deg/s. The ailerons saturate at 21.5 deg, and the sideslip stays below 1.5 deg.

# The FMU

The model's export markers define the FMU interface:

| | FMI names |
| --- | --- |
| inputs | `stick_pitch`, `stick_roll`, `pedals` (−1..1), `throttle` (0..1), `wind_n`, `wind_e`, `wind_d` (m/s) |
| outputs | position `north_m`, `east_m`, `alt_m`; quaternion `q_w..q_z` and `phi_deg`, `theta_deg`, `psi_deg`, `gamma_deg`; body rates and velocities; `tas_mps`, `cas_mps`, `mach`, `qbar_pa`, `alpha_deg`, `beta_deg`, `nz_g`, `ny_g`; surfaces; `power_pct`, `thrust_n`; `p_lim_dps`, `nz_cmd_g`, `limiter_active`, `departed`, `ground_contact` |
| parameters | the trim `vt0`, `h0`, `alpha0`, `el0`, `thtl0` (set together, from a trim), `xcg`, `mass`, and the law switches |

The `ExportInputPass` markers keep the test input in bg-sim. In the FMU the wire into them is cut and the host supplies the signal. The trim globals set the start state through expressions (the quaternion, the momentum and the engine power), and the FMU recomputes them at initialization.

Export it in the **Export** dialog with these globals as tunables, or run `examples/export_f16.jl`. The analysis picks RK4 with one 5 ms step per 5 ms communication step: the fastest closed-loop poles (the servos at 20.2 rad/s, the roll loop) are slow enough. One `doStep` takes about 3.5 µs, with no allocations after instantiation.

`examples/export_f16_check.py` drives the FMU in fmpy through four manoeuvres and compares it with bg-sim runs driven by the same inputs: a pitch doublet, a roll and roll back, a coordinated turn and a throttle step to full afterburner. The angles agree within 2e-3 deg and the altitude within 1 cm. The largest relative deviation is the engine power, 0.7 % of its peak for a moment: the FMU evaluates the switch at military power once per step.
